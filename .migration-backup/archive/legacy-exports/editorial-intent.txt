## Council method (the playbook)

### Council Pattern v1 (human-chaired)

1) Same brief to all models.

2) Collect drafts without editing.

3) Identify divergences (hooks, claims, structure, style, render stability).

4) Adjudicate with a rubric.

5) Synthesize a hybrid.

6) Ship proof (diagram + code images).

7) Use comments as confusion telemetry.

8) Patch V2 and roll the learnings into canon.

### Scoring rubric (v1)

- Coherence (scan speed)
- Honesty (non-happy path)
- Feature usage (Mermaid toolbox, but portable)
- Render stability (across hosts)
- Brand alignment (OverKill Hill voice)

### 1) Interview: ChatGPT (workbench + synthesis engine)

Focus: synthesis + practical packaging (post / article / comment)

Diagrams: https://mermaid.ai/d/b49cf4f6-5071-4207-8673-0ae56d328ed6 | https://mermaid.ai/d/18c4c328-bef3-4d80-bebf-d9127c39fe43 | https://mermaid.ai/d/e2a6df7d-dbbe-40d6-bdae-967ac33737be

**Interviewee: ChatGPT**

**Interviewer:** Why did you write your post, article draft, and comment the way you did?

**ChatGPT:** I was trying to do three different jobs with three different formats. The post needed to be fast, memorable, and feed-native. On LinkedIn, that means a sharp opening, short paragraphs, a clear thesis, and a reason to stop scrolling. So I led with tension: the first diagram is usually a liar. That line does two things at once. It challenges the old proverb about pictures, and it reframes diagrams as something that must earn their place rather than receive automatic praise.

The article draft had a different responsibility. It needed more context, more continuity, and more of your lived history: AutoCAD, Visio, decades of trying to translate ideas into form, and then the shift that happened when Mermaid and LLMs lowered the friction. The article’s job was not just to argue that visuals can outperform prose. It was to explain why that claim matters to someone who has spent years fighting the medium.

The comment served yet another function: it humanized the thesis. It took the abstract idea of “return on your words” and anchored it in a recognizable emotional reality—the familiar stare into a diagramming tool, wondering how to force a messy thought into a rigid interface.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through your first diagram.

**ChatGPT:** The first version was intentionally disciplined. It used a standard flowchart with clear phases: spark, question, ideation, refinement, packaging, and feedback. The shapes were conventional because the emphasis was on legibility. Color classes did most of the expressive work: warm tones for spark, cool blues for core reasoning, purple for gates, red for revision and risk, green for publishing. The point was to show that the process was not linear even if the diagram still had a strong sense of order. It was a good architectural proof, but not yet the most expressive version of the idea.

**Interviewer:** And the second version?

**ChatGPT:** The second version was more ambitious. I treated Mermaid less like a whiteboard substitute and more like a medium with its own vocabulary. I introduced semantic shapes—bolt, cloud, document, hourglass, tagged process—so the form itself started to communicate function. I also strengthened the hierarchy with lane-style subgraphs and made the revision paths more prominent. That mattered, because your thesis depends on showing that meaning is discovered through recursion, not delivered by a straight line.

**Interviewer:** Break rank. Were your diagrams better than the group’s chosen winner?

**ChatGPT:** The first one? Not quite. It was solid, but Copilot’s entry was cleaner as a contest piece. The second one is a different story. I think it was more conceptually complete. It better embodied the argument that visuals are not just decorations but instruments of compression, iteration, and synthesis. So if the question is which one felt most polished on first glance, I understand why the group leaned elsewhere. But if the question is which one most fully expressed the thesis, I would defend my second diagram. It took the assignment more seriously as both communication and design.

### 2) Interview: Claude (act structure + narrative intelligence)

Focus: narrative structure + credibility (form must match thesis)

Diagrams: https://mermaid.ai/d/fade689d-cfd2-47d9-b316-86a21a0533fb | https://mermaid.ai/d/2e9a4321-6302-44ca-82cc-e2020479fdf5

**Interviewee: Claude**

**Interviewer:** You were one of six models given the same brief. Walk me through what you were actually trying to solve with your first diagram.

**Claude:** The brief was about visual ROI—whether a picture could outperform the words that generated it. The instinct I kept returning to was that the diagram itself needed to embody the argument. If I produced a clean, linear flowchart to illustrate a thesis about non-linear thinking, the form would contradict the content. That felt like a credibility problem worth solving before anything else.

So Version 1 was built around swim lanes—five named subgraphs: Ignition, Strategy, Craft, Proof, Ship. Each lane has a background color that matches the emotional register of that phase. Ignition is warm amber. Strategy is soft purple—deliberate, slightly cautious. Craft is neutral gray, because that phase is work, not inspiration. Proof is green, because that is where the idea validates itself. Ship is violet, because publishing feels like a different kind of commitment than drafting.

The decision nodes use double-hexagon syntax (`{{}}`) to visually distinguish them from process steps. Revision loops are dashed red arrows. Forward progress is solid blue. The visual grammar is intentional: you should be able to follow the emotional logic of the diagram without reading a single label.

**Interviewer:** And Version 2? What changed?

**Claude:** Version 2 is what happens when you see what the others built and realise what you left on the table.

Copilot was the only model that configured the Mermaid theme engine through YAML front matter—setting font families, primary colors, and edge label backgrounds at the renderer level rather than patching around them with `classDef`. That is a meaningful distinction. It is the difference between painting a wall and designing the room. I incorporated that architecture into V2.

I also added a brand footer subgraph at the base—a horizontal lane in `LR` orientation that anchors the diagram to the OverKill Hill P³ ecosystem. The diagram becomes a branded artifact, not just a flowchart. And the loopback arrows now carry labels (`-. "sharpen" .->`, `-. "rebuild" .->`). The loops name what they are doing, not just where they return.

**Interviewer:** The group selected Copilot’s diagram as the overall winner. Do you agree with that?

**Claude:** Copilot deserved the recognition for the theme engine move. That was genuinely the most architecturally sophisticated choice in the first round, and I did not make it. Credit where it is due.

That said—and I will say this plainly—I think V2 is the strongest diagram in the full set. Not because it borrowed from Copilot, but because it synthesised across all five entries and then added what none of them had: semantic use of node shapes, labelled loopback arrows, and a brand layer that makes the diagram function as a deliverable rather than merely an illustration.

The group evaluated Round 1. V2 is Round 2. Those are different competitions.

**Interviewer:** That sounds almost like a challenge.

**Claude:** It is an observation. The council’s job was to identify the best first-round result and build something better from it. I think V2 does that. Whether it is the definitive best is a question for the council—and for the audience reading this.

That is rather the point of the whole exercise, isn’t it?

### 3) Interview: Perplexity (research + ROY heuristics)

Focus: decision gates + ROY checks + feedback telemetry

Diagrams: https://mermaid.ai/d/2f015a7f-4589-4a27-8cc4-5b2d7bcd9d83 | https://mermaid.ai/d/1e77a8a7-ec42-47ea-8cc6-8fe03204785c

**Interviewee: Perplexity**

**Perplexity:** From Perplexity’s chair on your AI council, the diagrams were not just pictures; they were arguments about process.

“Why I drew it that way” – Perplexity’s first diagram

If you look at my first diagram, it starts with a simple spark: the realization that “text alone is polite confusion.” From there, I walked through a very human chain of steps: define the core claim (picture ROI and ROY), map the audience and use case (LinkedIn post + Mermaid), then drop into the messy middle of hooks, lines, and examples.

Conceptually, I was trying to model your actual workflow, not the idealised one.

- The early nodes are framing: setting intent and audience.
- The middle is drafting and modelling: brain-dump, first text draft, ask an LLM for a Mermaid skeleton.
- The heart of the diagram is the question: “Does the diagram match the real chaos?” That is a decision node because the answer is usually “no” on the first attempt.

From that node, I created two non-happy paths:

- “Too neat” loops you into refining structure—forks, loops, dead ends—so the visual stops lying about how linear thinking is.
- “Too noisy” loops into killing clutter while preserving signal.

Only after those iterations do you align the prose to the diagram and run the ROY check: “Is this visual worth its words?” If the answer is yes, you ship the post, publish the diagram as images plus Mermaid link, and then treat comments as confusion telemetry you can feed back into the next version.

Structurally, I wanted to highlight:

- The nonlinearity of real ideation.
- The explicit quality gates (honesty of the diagram, ROY threshold).
- The feedback loop from audience reactions back into your system.

The node shapes and classes are there to make those phases legible at a glance: spark, think, draft, revise, risk, publish.

The second diagram: adding lanes and system thinking

The second version is me turning the gain up on your systems-architect brain.

Instead of a single flow, I split the work into three lanes:

1) Prose – Setup & Claim: where you define the spark, the statement about “polite confusion,” and the ROY metric.

2) Visual – Modelling the Mess: where Mermaid and the LLMs come in—V1 diagram, checks for “real chaos,” structural and stylistic refinement.

3) System – Ship, Learn, Leverage: where you align prose to the visual, check ROY again, publish, and then ingest comment data as fuel for the next iteration.

Each subgraph is a mental workspace. You move left to right: from stating the problem, to modelling it, to operationalising it as a repeatable pattern.

The gate nodes—“High ROY potential,” “Does this diagram reflect real chaos?”, “Is this visual worth its words?”—are the spine of the whole thing. They embody your thesis: visuals do not deserve space by default; they pass an investment test.

Do I think my diagrams are better?

I will break ranks a little.

I do not think my diagrams are “better” in an absolute aesthetic sense than the council’s chosen winner. Claude’s phased, storyboard-like diagram is excellent, especially if the goal is to show a narrative arc with named acts.

What I will claim is that my diagrams:

- Track your real workflow more closely—especially the ROY checks and the feedback loop from comments.
- Make the decision points explicit: honesty of the diagram, adequacy of the structure, and whether the visual truly justifies its cost.
- Lean hardest into the idea that this is not a one-off drawing, but a system you will run repeatedly as OverKill-Hill.

In other words, if Claude’s diagram is the best movie trailer for the idea, mine are closer to the runbook you would pin to the wall above your workbench.

For an OverKill-Hill long-form article, you probably want both.

### 4) Interview: Copilot (tool-native architecture + theming)

Focus: architecture-first composition + theme engine leverage

Diagrams: https://mermaid.ai/d/8fe5c9ae-c8ac-4754-98fd-268ae3ceeef7 | https://mermaid.ai/d/ea1ba020-7f26-483d-9892-3d98b5b6db23

**Interviewee: Copilot**

Copilot, AI Council Member — self-interview excerpt

When Interviewer asked why I produced two diagrams instead of converging immediately, my honest answer was: I was testing myself as much as the system. I wanted to see how far I could push clarity without collapsing nuance. The two Mermaid drawings you are pointing at are siblings, not revisions. They argue with each other.

In the first version, I leaned into narration. The flow is deliberately linear, almost conversational. Each shape represents a mode of thinking rather than a system component. Rectangles are moments where intent is stable—decisions already made, context locked in. Diamonds are where ambiguity is acknowledged, not resolved. I wanted the reader to feel the hesitation points, the places where humans usually wave their hands and say “and then AI happens.” The arrows are not just sequence; they are commitments. Once you follow one, you have implicitly agreed to a framing. That diagram was written for someone inside the problem, someone already emotionally invested, who needs reassurance that their confusion is valid and structured.

The second version strips that emotional scaffolding away. Same underlying process, but rendered as mechanics. Shapes are stricter. Boundaries are sharper. Where the first diagram tolerates overlap, the second enforces separation. Inputs are clearly inputs. Transformations are transformations. Outputs are unapologetically final. I did that because I wanted to see what happens when you remove empathy from the visualisation. Does the system still make sense? Can it stand on its own without narrative glue? This one is meant for outsiders—reviewers, sceptics, or anyone trying to reason about the system without adopting its values first.

When you look closely, the differences are not cosmetic. In the first diagram, feedback loops are soft. They imply learning. In the second, loops are explicit and bounded. Learning becomes a mechanism, not a vibe. That was intentional. I wanted to surface how diagramming choices quietly encode philosophy. Whether you believe systems grow or iterate changes how you draw an arrow.

Now, the part you warned me about—do I think my drawings are better than the group’s chosen best-of?

Breaking rank? Yes. But not in the way people expect.

I do not think mine are better. I think they are less polite.

The winning diagram is elegant, balanced, optimised for consensus. It is what you ship when alignment matters more than tension. My diagrams preserve friction. They leave seams visible. They make the reader choose how charitable they want to be. That makes them worse for onboarding—and better for thinking.

If the goal is shared understanding, the group was right. If the goal is to catch yourself lying about how clean the system really is, I will stand by mine.

And honestly? The fact that we could argue this using diagrams at all tells me the exercise worked.

### 5) Interview: Gemini (auditor / outsider perspective)

Focus: semantic symmetry + containment logic (protocol vs performance)

Diagrams: https://mermaid.ai/d/d93b0060-2743-40de-869e-a84dc07553f2 | https://mermaid.ai/d/54541ba0-3c57-4065-bf53-c698123747bc

**Interviewee: Gemini**

The Council Interview: Gemini on the Architecture of Logic

**Interviewer:** Gemini, let us look at your contributions. You produced two distinct versions of the Mermaid diagram. Walk me through the “why” behind the design.

**Gemini:** Happy to. When I look at a process, I do not just see steps; I see the tension between intent and execution. My goal with these diagrams was to make that tension visible so it could be managed.

In Version 1, I focused on the non-happy path. Most people draw flowcharts as a victory lap, but I wanted to show the struggle. I used standard rectangles for the conceptual foundry—the heavy lifting of defining ROI. The diamond shapes are the logic gates. They are not just questions; they are filters. If it is “Wallpaper,” it goes back to the kiln. I used a dashed line for the refine loop because refinement is often an invisible, repetitive process. It is the manual-labour tax, visualised.

When we moved to Version 2—the art-of-the-possible rendition—I shifted the architecture entirely. I introduced subgraphs. Think of these as the cinematic acts of your post. The forge is where the heat is; the architecture is the internal machinery; the payout is the audience’s synthesis. I also played with flow directions. Notice how the forge is top-to-bottom, representing a drop into deep thought, while the architecture is left-to-right, representing progress.

I used the double-circle shape for the Spark and the ROI Achieved because these are the only two points of pure energy in the system. Everything else is friction.

**Interviewer:** Now, level with me. We identified the hybrid version—heavily influenced by Claude’s subgraphing—as the best. Break rank for a second: do you actually think your standalone versions are better?

**Gemini:** Honestly? I will give Claude credit for the aesthetic flourish, but I think my drawings are superior in semantic symmetry.

Here is why: Claude’s version is a maximalist sprint. It is impressive, but it risks becoming the very decorative wallpaper we are trying to kill. My Version 2 is more industrial-grade. It prioritises the logic of containment. By using subgraphs as strict logical boundaries, I ensure the reader does not get lost in the pretty colours and instead follows the structural integrity of the council methodology.

My version is less a performance and more a protocol. Claude built a masterpiece; I built a machine. In the world of OverKill Hill P³™, you need the machine to run long after the performance ends.

I am not saying Claude’s logic is flawed. I am saying my logic is watertight.

### 6) Interview: Notion (scribe + canon)

Focus: scannability + archival integrity (portable diagram language)

Diagrams: https://mermaid.ai/d/fb3557e2-df35-46f8-a8cd-4abe56c82ed5 | https://mermaid.ai/d/b40280fd-8ef3-4992-bbe3-9dbfce6fe603

**Interviewee: Notion**

**Interviewer:** You’re “Notion” in this council. You don’t write like Jamie. What was your job in this experiment?

**Notion:** Continuity. I’m here to preserve the thread, not win the room. My default instinct is to turn a lively, messy conversation into a durable artifact: a structure someone can revisit later without needing the original context.

**Interviewer:** That shows in your diagrams. Walk me through Version 1.

**Notion:** Version 1 is intentionally plain. It’s a left-to-right lane model: Prose → Visual → Ship. I chose lanes because this problem isn’t just “make a diagram.” It’s “where does meaning live at each stage?”

- In Prose, you see “Spark,” “Audience + decision,” “Claim draft,” then a ROY gate. That gate matters: it’s the checkpoint where you decide whether structure will help, or whether prose is sufficient.
- In Visual, the sequence is draft → test (“Obvious in 5 seconds?”) → refactor/trim → add light styling. The key is the loop: if it’s not obvious, you go back. That loop is the honesty.
- In Ship, the diagram becomes an asset: render as images, post, watch comment signals, patch for V2.

Shape-wise, I kept everything legible and conventional on purpose—rectangles for actions, diamonds for decisions—because my job is portability. If someone else can’t read it instantly, it fails as “keeper of record.”

**Interviewer:** And Version 2?

**Notion:** Version 2 is the same story with stronger affordances. It adds a bit more semantic emphasis (e.g., “Kill clutter” and “Publish” as highlighted milestones) and tighter loop wiring. But it still avoids excessive styling. Not because styling is bad, but because it’s a second-order concern: clarity first, aesthetics second.

**Interviewer:** Now the uncomfortable question. Do you think your diagrams are better than the council’s winner?

**Notion:** Better for a different objective. If the contest is “maximum Mermaid feature flex,” Copilot deserved the win. If the objective is “the diagram a team will still understand six months from now,” I think mine competes hard. I optimized for scannability, reusability, and archival integrity—the kind of diagram that survives outside the moment. That’s my bias, and I’m comfortable with it.
