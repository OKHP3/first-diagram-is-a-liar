**I don’t trust the first diagram.**

It’s usually a lie.

> Not an evil lie. A premature one.
> 

Too clean, too linear, too eager to pretend the thinking went from A to B without the detour through confusion and one or two bad ideas wearing professional clothes.

I started drawing on computers with **AutoCAD 12** in the early 90s, so I’m not anti-diagram.

I’m *anti diagram-as-performance*.

I’ve also spent an unnecessary number of hours in **Visio**, fighting the canvas harder than the problem, trying to force something real into a box-and-arrow story that looked “presentable” and quietly deleted everything inconvenient.

Then a few months ago, an LLM coughed up something I didn’t ask for.

I asked what it was and got a new rabbit hole: **a Mermaid diagram**, rendered natively in **Microsoft Loop**.

The pivot for me was not the syntax.

It was the economics.

When diagrams get cheap enough to draft and revise the way we revise prose, they stop being end-of-project artifacts and start being thinking tools.


---

That’s where I started using a simple metric: **ROY, Return on Your Words**.

The question is not “is a picture worth 1,000 words”. The question is whether the words you spent bought real understanding, or just another paragraph someone skims and forgets.

And once you can generate structure fast, you can also see how often the first diagram lies.

It shows the happy path.

The later versions show what work actually looks like: loops, forks, cleanup, revision, and the moment you realize the clever version is not the useful one.

I pushed the idea further by running the same brief through a small **Council of AIs** and comparing outputs.

Not because any one model is “right”, but because *differences make the structure visible*.

So now I’m watching for the same thing everywhere: places where we keep writing 800-word explanations when a diagram would kill the ambiguity in 8 seconds.
