## Assessment

There is enough substantive material to begin the final article now. Finalization still needs a focused literature and claim-verification pass. The main work is consolidation, evidence qualification, and a stronger business operating model. More installments would add little value at this point.

The strongest contribution is a practical method for making shared understanding inspectable: capture context, produce a model, expose disagreement, apply human judgment, revise, and retain the knowledge behind the picture. ROY supplies the question; the Council supplies a method; V1/V2 supply the case; the later process-documentation work supplies the business consequence.

The current collection is not ready to be presented as a validated academic study. It can become a rigorous practitioner case study with an explicit method, traceable artifacts, limitations, and actionable propositions. Its limitations do not require another long research project before publication. Unsupported claims can be narrowed or removed. New controlled evaluation would be required only to retain stronger claims of superiority, causal impact, or measured productivity improvement.

**Recommended final length: 9,500 words**, including a short reference section. Preserve the title. Use a business-facing subtitle such as **How to Turn AI Diagrams into Shared Understanding and Maintainable Process Knowledge**. Keep the release identifier in metadata and publication history. The main narrative does not need v0.x labels, promises of the next installment, or the director's-cut metaphor.

## The final argument and its boundaries

### Working thesis

When diagram generation becomes inexpensive, the scarce resource shifts to establishing and maintaining shared understanding. Text-native diagrams, structured comparison, and human adjudication can make assumptions easier to inspect and revise. Their business value depends on source fidelity, reader performance, and a maintained process record that contains the knowledge a diagram necessarily compresses.

This thesis connects the whole collection without requiring a claim that one tool always wins, more models always help, or the original experiment established financial return.

### Questions the article should answer

1. When does a diagram reduce the reader's reconstruction work, and when does it merely hide complexity?
2. What did comparing and revising the V1/V2 artifacts reveal in this particular exercise?
3. How should a business team turn that experience into a repeatable, proportionate review method?
4. What must remain in the underlying process record so that visual compression does not destroy useful knowledge?
5. What evidence would show that the method helped, and what would cause the team to stop using it?

### What the case can support

It can demonstrate an inspectable workflow, differences among preserved outputs, the author's round-specific selections, peer-informed revision, and practical design lessons. It can report documented audience activity with appropriate denominator and sampling limits. It can propose a business operating model grounded in those observations.

It does not currently establish causal gains in comprehension, general model rankings, a statistically representative audience preference, a universal advantage from councils, or production readiness of the proposed near-real-time process loop. A model's retrospective explanation is itself generated evidence requiring interpretation.

## Business strategy and tactical implementation

### A proportionate operating model

| Stage | Accountable role | Work product | Decision gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frame | Business/process owner | Decision to support, scope, users, cost of error, known exceptions | Is a visual or comparison likely to help this decision? |
| Capture | Analyst with subject-matter expert | Narrative record with actors, inputs, controls, evidence, unknowns, and stable IDs | Has the person doing the work confirmed the record? |
| Model | Diagram author / assisted drafting tool | First diagram linked to the narrative | Does it render, and can each consequential element be traced to a source? |
| Challenge | Reviewers chosen for different concerns | Independent observations, alternatives, and uncertainty list | Did the review find a meaningful omission or merely a stylistic preference? |
| Adjudicate | Domain owner with human editor | Accepted/rejected changes with reasons | Are unresolved errors and exceptions visible? |
| Validate | Intended reader plus subject-matter expert | Task answers, exception recognition, correction time, and comprehension feedback | Can a reader use it correctly without a guided tour? |
| Publish | Document owner | Approved prose, visual, source, captions, date, and evidence links | Is the artifact fit for its stated audience and purpose? |
| Maintain | Named process owner | Change triggers, review date, dependency/source links | Does a process change require revision of narrative, diagram, or both? |

For a low-risk, familiar workflow, the same person can hold several roles and the Council may be unnecessary. For consequential or ambiguous work, separate drafting from acceptance. Suggested stopping rule: stop generating alternatives when the domain owner has resolved material disagreements and another pass yields only stylistic variation. This is a proposed operating rule, not a finding measured in the original exercise.

### One worked example, explicitly illustrative

Use the existing public purchase-request example to show the mechanism. Start with “Submit → Review → Approve.” Then expose missing conditions: budget authority, absent approver, missing evidence, rejection, return for clarification, duplicate request, and escalation. Link the “Review request” node to the narrative that defines its criteria and authority. Ask the intended reader to route three ordinary cases and two exceptions, then correct the diagram and record together.

Do not claim these results happened in the Council experiment. A constructed example can teach the method without being represented as a field deployment or client outcome.

Also include one counterexample from the actual archive: Claude V1 combines a question about a happy path with a question about fidelity to real ideation in one decision node. Its yes/no arrows become ambiguous because those questions can have opposite answers. This is a concrete way to show that a favored artifact can still hide an error. Preserve the original and annotate it; adding loops alone does not make a diagram truthful.

### A usable value-measurement card

- **Decision/task:** what the reader must correctly understand or do.
- **Baseline:** current prose or current process diagram, with the same underlying facts.
- **Primary quality check:** correct decisions and identified exceptions, scored against a subject-matter expert's reference.
- **Effort measures:** preparation, review, correction, and reader task time, recorded separately.
- **Maintenance measure:** time and errors when a known process change is applied.
- **Business interpretation:** translate time and rework into cost only with explicit assumptions and the organization's own rates.
- **Design guard:** compare similar readers/tasks; change presentation order where practical; acknowledge learning effects and small samples.
- **Decision:** retain the new approach only if it improves a relevant outcome without unacceptable loss of correctness or maintenance cost.

An optional proposed business calculation is `net time benefit = reader/rework time avoided - added author/reviewer/maintenance time`, over a named period and audience. It can be negative. Keep that calculation distinct from ROY's rhetorical ratio and from the tutorial's bounded teaching score. Do not insert invented savings, percentages, or statistical significance.
