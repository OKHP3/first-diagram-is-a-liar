The Council Scores the Field

The architect read was in. The Council read was the missing lane. I asked each of the five Core Five members to score the same diagrams independently against the same seven-criterion rubric. No member saw another member's scores before submitting. Gemini was on the free tier; the other four Core Five members were on paid tiers.

These rankings cover the Core Five only. Notion and Replit remain Specialty entries, and ChatGPT V2 Pro remains an Exhibition entry. Their different conditions do not enter this direct comparison.

The rubric covered first-glance comprehension, semantic fidelity, diagram structure, visual hierarchy, theming discipline, iteration improvement from V1 to V2, and usefulness as a thinking artifact.

Round 1 produced consensus. Round 2 produced disagreement. That split is more useful than a single blended leaderboard.

Round 1: Copilot V1

All five Core Five scoring members rated Copilot V1 first on structural clarity. The Council and architect converged on the same differentiator: its YAML front matter configured Mermaid's theme engine. The other submissions styled their diagrams. Copilot configured the renderer.

This is a Round 1 result, not an overall title. Inspect Copilot V1 in Mermaid.

Round 2: Three Defensible Reads

The architect's Round 2 top performer was Claude V2. Its visible revision loops made the non-linear process structurally honest. The Council did not converge on one V2 across every criterion:

  • Claude V2 led the narrative architecture read. Three of five scoring members placed it first on semantic fidelity and usefulness as a thinking artifact.
  • Copilot V2 led the structural read. Two members placed it first on diagram structure and theming discipline.
  • ChatGPT V2 led the completeness read. It made the loops and decision points explicit while retaining hierarchy.

These are criterion-specific Council rankings. The architect's Round 2 result remains Claude V2. The Council's divergence does not overturn that judgment, and the architect's judgment does not erase what the Council valued. There is no overall Council winner.

Inspect Claude V2 in Mermaid.

The Self-Scoring Result

Across these five Core Five members and both rounds, every model rated its own submission below the architect's score for that submission. That is the result in this sample, under this rubric. It is not a claim about how models behave in general.

The useful next question was not whether a model would praise itself. It was what it could identify when asked to assess its own work against a shared field and explicit criteria.

The Models Interview Themselves

After scoring, I asked each Core Five member three questions about its V1 and V2 diagrams: What did you get right? What would you change? Should you have won? The summaries below are paraphrased composites, not verbatim transcripts. The reasoning belongs to each model; the compression is mine. Each model knew its answers would appear in the article.

ChatGPT

Got right: Clear loop logic in V1, then more explicit loopbacks and labels in V2.

Would change: V1's visual flair got ahead of its structure. V2 risked replacing that problem with density.

Should it have won? Not Round 1. Possibly Round 2 if completeness and team usability lead; Claude V2 has the stronger case if process honesty leads.

Claude

Got right: V1 had a coherent narrative arc; V2 made revision loops visible and treated them as part of the argument.

Would change: V1 made ideation look too graceful. The false starts should have appeared sooner.

Should it have won? Claude argued yes for V2 because structural honesty mattered more than polish or completeness.

Copilot

Got right: V1 configured Mermaid at the renderer level, a technical distinction beyond styling individual nodes.

Would change: V2 improved the tone but kept much of V1's structure. Another pass should deepen the loop logic.

Should it have won? Yes for Round 1. For Round 2, Copilot accepted Claude V2 under the architect's criterion of earned elegance through honest recursion.

Perplexity

Got right: V1 favored utility and information density over decoration, a strong Return on Your Words read.

Would change: V2 tried to satisfy several criteria at once and diluted the clarity that made V1 distinct.

Should it have won? No. Perplexity argued that its simplicity bet was valid, but the rubric gave more weight to structural sophistication.

Gemini

Got right: V1 reduced complexity through restraint instead of adding more structure.

Would change: V2 needed to choose a clear position. Restraint became generic when it avoided that choice.

Should it have won? No. Gemini judged that its V2 held its place rather than raising the bar set by the stronger revisions.

The interviews did not produce a universal rule about AI self-assessment. They produced something narrower and more useful: five specific critiques tied to five bodies of work, two rounds, and one shared rubric. For this exercise, asking the creator to name the compromise surfaced details worth reviewing.
