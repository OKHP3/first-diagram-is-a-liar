## LinkedIn article update

### v0.9: The Diagram Became the Happy Accident

One reason this article series took me so long was not a lack of time or interest. I was caught in a mental whirlwind. Every day brought something new, then something deeper, then another turn in the maze that made yesterday’s conclusion feel incomplete.

The more I learned about Mermaid, the less interested I became in the novelty of making a cool picture. Yes, I could coach an AI into generating a diagram. That was useful, but it was not the part that kept pulling me back. The real shift was that the words and the diagram could become the same artifact.

For most of my life, diagramming meant writing or hearing an explanation, translating it into shapes, routing lines, and arranging a canvas until the picture looked close enough to the meaning. Then the words changed. The process evolved. The diagram fell behind. Eventually the description and the picture became competing versions of the truth, each waiting for somebody with enough time and patience to reconcile them.

Mermaid changes that equation because the source remains text. When the structured words change, the visual can be regenerated. That is the first multiplier: the diagram does not have to be a frozen translation of last month’s understanding. It can remain attached to the source that produced it.

Business processes exposed the harder problem. Most process diagrams are made of tasks, decisions, handoffs, and events, but the box labeled “Review request” does not explain how the review actually works. It does not carry the exceptions, judgment calls, controls, workarounds, dependencies, or consequences that live behind those two words.

When you sit with someone who knows a recurring job, their link in the chain is rarely a tidy symbol. It may represent years of experience and dozens of small decisions they make without announcing them. They know which exception is harmless, which handoff always breaks, who needs context before the request arrives, and what evidence makes the next person trust the work. Compressing all of that into one shape can become another kind of lie.

That human knowledge deserves to be captured at the depth the person is willing to share. It also has to survive outside the conversation. If the process may eventually be used by an AI agent, a diagram is nowhere near enough. The agent needs the narrative, actors, inputs, decisions, rules, controls, measures, exceptions, evidence, escalation paths, and handoff material that make a runbook or SOP executable.

That realization changed the project. I started by trying to create a Mermaid renderer for a readable `bpmn-beta` syntax. I am still working on that renderer, and other contributors in the Mermaid community are advancing BPMN support faster than I am in some areas. The larger contribution grew beside it: BP-SKILL, a suite of 15 Agent Skills for collecting, structuring, challenging, documenting, visualizing, and handing off a business process.

Those skills share a Process Narrative Specification so the narrative and the visual model can refer to the same process record. They are written to the portable Agent Skills convention rather than one product’s private prompt format. The practical aim is simple: preserve the depth of the human explanation, then create views of that knowledge for the people and systems that need it.

The workflow I am chasing starts with a real conversation. It might happen in Teams, Google Meet, or any setting where an assisted transcript can be collected. The transcript becomes structured process documentation. The major steps become a Mermaid model, with each task acting more like a section heading than a substitute for the detail. Links can take a reviewer back to the relevant narrative, evidence, or supporting document.

That visual then becomes a review surface. The person who actually performs the work can see what was captured while the conversation is still fresh. They can challenge the sequence, expose a missing exception, correct an invented handoff, and send the record back through the loop. As the process documentation changes, the diagram can change with it.

That full near-real-time loop is still a direction, not a finished production system. The public project today is a technical prototype, a bounded descriptive subset of BPMN, and a portable 15-skill process-documentation suite. It is not full BPMN 2.0 conformance, BPMN XML round-tripping, or an executable process engine.

The renderer began as the project. The diagram became the happy accident: a visible checkpoint that helps a human decide whether the system understood the work.

The renderer makes the process visible. The skills are meant to keep the knowledge alive.

Project, prototype, skills, and current boundaries: https://overkillhill.com/projects/bpmn-for-mermaid/

## Post

One reason this article series took me so long was not time.

It was the mental whirlwind.

Every day I learned something new, went a little deeper down the hole, and found another turn in the maze.

The more I learned about Mermaid, the more captivated I became. Not because it could make cool pictures. Not because I could “vibe diagram.”

Because the words and the diagram could become the same artifact.

For most of my life, diagramming meant translating words into shapes, routing lines, and arranging a canvas. Then the words changed. The process evolved. The diagram fell behind. Eventually both versions became irrelevant.

Mermaid changes that equation. Keep the structure as text, and the visual can be regenerated as the source changes.

That was the first multiplier.

Then business processes exposed the harder problem.

A person's link in the chain is not one symbol and three words. It can contain years of judgment, exceptions, handoffs, workarounds, and consequences. If someone gives you hours of their experience, compressing it into a tidy box can become another kind of lie.

And if the goal is to make that process usable by an AI agent, a diagram is nowhere near enough. You need the narrative, rules, decisions, controls, measures, SOPs, and handoff material behind it.

So BPMN for Mermaid changed shape.

It started as a renderer for a readable, text-first `bpmn-beta` subset. It grew into BP-SKILL: 15 agent skills for collecting, structuring, challenging, documenting, visualizing, and handing off a business process.

The diagram became the happy accident.

The bigger idea is a review loop: conversation becomes structured process documentation; the major steps become a visual model; the person who actually knows the work can see what was captured, challenge it, and correct it while the knowledge is still fresh.

That full near-real-time loop is still a direction, not a finished production system. The public project today is a technical prototype, a bounded BPMN descriptive subset, and a portable 15-skill process-documentation suite.

The renderer still matters.

But the larger offering is helping preserve the human knowledge the diagram is supposed to represent.

See the project and its current boundaries: https://overkillhill.com/projects/bpmn-for-mermaid/
